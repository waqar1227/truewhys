# Sources — A-017 Why Ice Cubes Are Cloudy
| Claim (lines) | Sources |
|---|---|
| Water holds dissolved air; ice holds far less, so air comes out of solution as water freezes (L01–L02) | Purdue University MSE "Got Materials?: Clear ice" (Yoder), https://engineering.purdue.edu/MSE/AboutUs/GotMaterials/Other/yoder.html ; Indiana Public Media, A Moment of Science "The secret behind getting clear ice cubes", https://indianapublicmedia.org/amomentofscience/the-secret-behind-getting-clear-ice-cubes.php ; Cook's Illustrated "What is clear ice and how can I make it at home?", https://cooksillustrated.com/articles/2090-what-is-clear-ice-and-how-can-i-make-it-at-home |
| Tray cubes freeze from the outside in; air and impurities are pushed to the centre, which freezes last and traps them (L03–L05) | Purdue (above) ; A Moment of Science (above) ; Science ABC, https://www.scienceabc.com/eyeopeners/why-are-some-ice-cubes-cloudy-while-others-are-clear |
| Trapped tiny bubbles scatter light → white/cloudy look (L06) | Purdue (above) ; Science ABC (above) |
| A lake freezes from the top down (one direction), letting air escape → clear ice; directional freezing from one side makes clear ice (L07–L08) | Cook's Illustrated (above) ; A Moment of Science (above) ; Hackaday "The art and science of making beautiful transparent ice" (2023), https://hackaday.com/2023/05/24/the-art-and-science-of-making-beautiful-transparent-ice |
Not claimed: boiling as the fix (it helps only a little), minerals as the main cause, bubble counts.
