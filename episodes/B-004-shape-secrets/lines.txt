O02|0|He snips the edge off a silver coin. Just a sliver. Then the next coin. And the next.

O06|0|Because almost every strange shape around you was a fix for problems somebody had.
C101|1|So why do so many coins have tiny ridges on the edge?
C102|1|Most coins in English pockets were old ones, hammered out by hand. They were never perfectly round, so nobody noticed a missing edge.
C103|1|Clipping was so serious it counted as treason. You could be hanged for it.
C104|1|In 1696, England called in the old hammered coins and replaced them with machine-made ones, with patterned edges.
C105|1|Now, if someone trimmed a coin, the pattern was gone. Anyone could see it in a second.
C106|1|Newton ran the Mint's hunt for coin criminals. He collected the evidence against one of London's most famous forgers, William Chaloner.
C107|1|In 1699, Chaloner was hanged.
C108|1|Today, an American dime or quarter has no silver in it at all. There's nothing worth shaving.
C109|1|So why keep the ridges? Partly tradition... and partly your fingers. A dime and a penny are almost the same size. With your eyes closed, the ridges tell you which is which.
C201|2|Now look down. Why is almost every manhole cover round?
C202|2|Try this with a square one. Turn it on its side, and the long diagonal fits right through the hole.
C203|2|Clang. A heavy iron lid falls into the sewer... maybe onto a worker.
C204|2|A circle is the same width in every direction. However you turn it, it can never fall through its own hole.
C205|2|A round lid is also easy to roll, instead of lifting it. And it drops back in at any angle.

C207|2|Engineers just found the circle easier to make.
C301|3|Now, the stop sign. Why does it have eight sides?
C302|3|In 1923, a group of American road engineers had a strange idea: the more sides a sign has, the more dangerous the place.
C303|3|A circle, with endless sides, meant a railway crossing: the deadliest spot of all.
C304|3|Eight sides meant stop. A diamond meant caution. A square meant care. A rectangle was just information.
C305|3|And here's the twist. For about thirty years, most American stop signs weren't red.
C306|3|They were yellow, with black letters.
C307|3|Red paint faded in the sun. A pale pink sign is easy to miss, and that's dangerous.
C308|3|Only in 1954, when paint and reflective material lasted, did the stop sign finally turn red.
C401|4|Next, the grocery bag. Why does a paper bag have a flat, square bottom?
C402|4|Early paper bags were like big envelopes. Flat, pointed at the bottom... and they fell over.
C403|4|In the late 1860s, Margaret Knight worked in a paper bag factory in Massachusetts.
C404|4|She built a wooden model of a machine that folded and glued a bag with a flat bottom.
C405|4|She took it to a machine shop to have it made in iron. And a man named Charles Annan saw it... and patented it first.
C406|4|In court, Annan's argument was simple: a woman couldn't possibly design a machine like this.
C407|4|Knight brought her drawings, her notes and her diary.
C408|4|In 1871, she won. The patent was hers. And the bag that stands up on its own is still her design.
C501|5|Now count the teeth on a bottle cap. Twenty-one. Almost every time.
C502|5|In the 1880s, soda and beer bottles were sealed with corks and wire. The gas leaked, and the drink went flat.
C503|5|In 1892, in Baltimore, William Painter patented a metal cap with a crimped skirt, like a little crown.
C504|5|His first caps had twenty-four teeth.
C505|5|But over the years, makers settled on twenty-one. Enough to hold the fizz in, and still easy to pop off with an opener.
C506|5|Painter even sold the bottle opener.
C507|5|And one of his salesmen got some famous advice. Painter told him: invent something that people use once, throw away, and buy again.
C508|5|That salesman was King Gillette. A few years later, he had the idea for the throwaway razor blade.
P01|8|Next, something hiding behind your sofa. Why do the flat prongs on an American plug have little holes?
P02|8|The first answer was clever. Some early sockets had tiny bumps inside that clicked into the holes, so the plug wouldn't slip out.
P03|8|But open most sockets today, and there's no bump at all. The prongs are just gripped by springy metal.
P04|8|So what are the holes for now? The official standard answers in one dry line: the hole is optional, and it's for manufacturing.
P05|8|The holes help hold the metal prongs in place while the plastic plug is moulded around them.
P06|8|Sometimes the strangest shape has the most boring answer.
C601|6|Last one. It's on your plate. Why do crackers have little holes?
C602|6|It isn't decoration. When cracker dough bakes, the water inside turns to steam.
C603|6|With nowhere to go, the steam blows bubbles, and the cracker puffs up like a pillow.
C604|6|So bakers poke holes into the dough before it goes into the oven. It's called docking.
C605|6|The steam escapes through the holes, and the cracker stays flat and crisp.
C606|6|Too few holes, and it puffs. Too many, and it turns hard and dry. The pattern is measured, not random.
