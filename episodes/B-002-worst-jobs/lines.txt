L08|1|Number eight: the ale conner.
L09|1|At first, this sounds like the best job in town.
L10|1|In medieval England, an ale conner was chosen by the local court to walk from alehouse to alehouse...
L11|1|and taste the ale.
L12|1|If a brewer sold weak ale, short measures, or charged too much...
L13|1|the ale conner could drag them before the court to be fined.
L14|1|So why is it on this list?
L15|1|Because the people you tested were your neighbours. And nobody likes the person who reports them.
L16|1|You may have heard that ale conners sat in a puddle of ale in leather trousers, to see if they stuck to the bench.
L17|1|Beer historians say that story is almost certainly a myth.
L18|1|But some towns still keep the ceremony alive today... just for fun.
L19|2|Number seven: the Groom of the Stool.
L20|2|In the Tudor court, the "stool" was the king's toilet.
L21|2|And the groom was the servant who looked after the king while he used it.
L22|2|Sounds terrible. But here's the twist.
L23|2|Because he was alone with the king every day, the groom became one of the most trusted men in the country.
L24|2|Under Henry the Eighth, the groom ran the king's private rooms, handled his money, and heard his secrets.
L25|2|Nobles fought for this job.
L26|2|But being that close to Henry was dangerous.
L27|2|In 1536, his groom, Sir Henry Norris, was accused of a romance with Queen Anne Boleyn.||In fifteen thirty-six, his groom, Sir Henry Norris, was accused of a romance with Queen Anne Boleyn.
L28|2|The charges were almost certainly false. He was executed anyway.
L29|3|Number six: the toad eater.
L30|3|In the 1600s, travelling quacks sold fake medicine at fairs.||In the sixteen hundreds, travelling quacks sold fake medicine at fairs.
L31|3|And many brought an assistant.
L32|3|People believed every toad was deadly poisonous.
L33|3|So, the story goes, the assistant would swallow a toad in front of the crowd...
L34|3|pretend to fall down, twisting in pain...
L35|3|and then the quack would give him a sip of "miracle medicine".
L36|3|He'd jump up, cured! And the crowd would rush to buy a bottle.
L37|3|This job left its mark on our language.
L38|3|A person who flatters someone powerful became a "toad-eater"...
L39|3|and later, a "toady". A word we still use today.
L40|4|Number five: the sin eater.
L41|4|This one is spooky.
L42|4|In the 1680s, a writer named John Aubrey described a custom in parts of Wales and the English border.||In the sixteen eighties, a writer named John Aubrey described a custom in parts of Wales and the English border.
L43|4|When someone died, a poor person was hired to come to the house.

L50|5|Number four: the saltpetre man.
L51|5|To make gunpowder, England needed saltpetre.
L52|5|And one of the best places to find it... was soil soaked with animal waste.
L53|5|Like the floor of your stable, your pigeon house, or your cellar.
L54|5|So the crown gave saltpetre men the right to dig.
L55|5|In 1627, a royal order even let them dig up the floors of stables and cellars...||In sixteen twenty-seven, a royal order even let them dig up the floors of stables and cellars...
L56|5|and told owners to leave the floors as loose earth, so they'd be easier to dig next time.
L57|5|Imagine a stranger arriving with a shovel, and you're not allowed to say no.
L58|5|Saltpetre men were some of the most hated people in the country.
L59|6|Number three: the fuller.
L60|6|Fresh wool cloth is greasy and loose. It has to be cleaned and thickened.
L61|6|For centuries, one way to do it was with stale urine.
L62|6|Collected, left to stand for days, and poured into a big tub with the cloth.
L63|6|Then the fuller climbed in... and stamped on the cloth with his bare feet. For hours.
L64|6|The ammonia in old urine helped strip the grease and whiten the wool.
L65|6|Over time, water-powered fulling mills took over much of the stamping.
L66|6|But the job left a mark too. Fuller, Tucker and Walker are all English surnames that come from this work.
L67|7|Number two: the tanner.
L68|7|Turning animal skins into leather was one of the smelliest jobs in history.
L69|7|Skins were scraped, soaked for months in pits, and softened with a paste made of dung.
L70|7|Dog dung and pigeon droppings were favourites.
L71|7|The smell was so bad that towns often pushed tanners outside the walls, next to a river.
L72|7|Around 1425, the brewers of Colchester complained that the tanners were soaking hides in the river they used for brewing.||Around fourteen twenty-five, the brewers of Colchester complained that the tanners were soaking hides in the river they used for brewing.
L73|7|Leather was everywhere: shoes, belts, buckets, saddles. Someone had to make it.
L74|7|But almost nobody wanted to live next door.
L75|8|And number one... the gong farmer.
L76|8|A "gong" was a toilet. And a gong farmer emptied the pits underneath them.
L77|8|By hand. With a shovel and a bucket.

L79|8|The pits had no fresh air, and the fumes could knock a worker out.
